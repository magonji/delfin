How the Budget works

Overview
You don't type a monthly target — you build it. The target is the sum of your fixed expenses and your planned expenses for the month, and the page then measures the real spending against it.
Key figures
Target — fixed expenses plus planned expenses for this month.
Spent — actual transactions this month, plus money moved into a savings pot for a future bill.
Committed — fixed expenses still unpaid, planned budgets not yet spent, and any shortfall in the month's savings pots.
Available — target minus spent minus committed.
Fixed expenses
Anything with a set amount and a rhythm: rent, insurance, a subscription, a transfer to a loan or savings account. Give it a first date and how often it repeats, and a payee so payment can be detected. A one-off you only expect this month goes here too — set it to repeat "once".
Anything repeating less often than monthly is prorated: a £600 bill every six months is budgeted at £100 a month, starting straight away even if the first charge is still in the future. That is what the month's target uses.
Mark an amount as estimated when you don't know it yet — an MOT, say.
✓ Paid — a matching transaction was found
○ Pending — counts as committed
≡ Prorated — tracked by its savings pot, not one by one
Click the status to set it by hand when the detector gets it wrong; click again to hand it back.
Set aside for future bills
Prorated expenses pointing at the same savings account are grouped into one pot, and the pot is compared against what you actually transferred there this month. Checking it per account rather than per bill is what makes a single lump-sum transfer work.
Expected income
What you expect to earn this month. Its total is what expected savings is measured against.
Planned expenses
The variable spending you budget for: groceries, eating out, fuel. Give each one the accounts and categories that represent it, and the bar fills as the month's transactions land. Subcategories of a chosen category count too.
Repeating and editing
Everything repeats by its own rhythm, so next month is already laid out. Editing an amount changes this month and every month ahead — months already closed keep the figures they were budgeted with, which is what makes the history worth auditing.
Calendar
Each day splits its spending into the four kakeibo buckets — Essentials, Indulgences, Culture, Unexpected — each in a fixed position within the cell. Nothing is drawn for a bucket with no spending. Click a figure for that day's transactions in that bucket. What the method is.
Spending in a category you haven't classified shows as a grey figure with a ?. Map your categories to clear it.
The strip along the foot of each cell is the pace: your planned expenses spread evenly across the month. Green means on pace, ochre up to twice it, red beyond. Bills are excluded — they're already set aside. Dots mark bills falling that day, green if paid.

The kakeibo method

Kakeibo (家計簿, "household account book") is a Japanese method from 1904: you write down what you spend, day by day, and sort it into four kinds of spending. The point isn't the arithmetic — your bank already does that — it's the question you answer as you write each entry: what kind of want was this?
Fixed costs and savings come off the top; the four buckets are for the money that passes through your hands day to day, and that's where the pattern shows.
The four buckets

Essentials

Routine, unavoidable spending — living costs you'd struggle to go without.

Everyday food: butcher, fishmonger, greengrocer, the weekly shop · Chemist: medicines, toiletries · Transport: fuel, taxis, tolls, train, bus and metro fares · Children and parents: school supplies, special diets · Pets: vet, food, grooming

Indulgences

Things you don't need to live, bought because they're a pleasure.

Bars and cafés: coffee, beer, soft drinks · Restaurants: meals out with friends, work or family · Takeaways and fast food · Nightlife · Clothes: garments, bags, shoes, accessories · Cosmetics: make-up, creams, perfume

Culture

Leisure that also leaves you with something — it enriches rather than just entertains.

Books and music · Live events: concerts, theatre, festivals · Film: cinema, streaming subscriptions · Press: newspapers, magazines

Unexpected

What fits nowhere else: exceptional events calling for a one-off outlay.

Travel: transport, hotels · Gifts: birthdays, Christmas, anniversaries · Repairs: home or car · Household goods: furniture, kitchenware, tools · Electronics: computers, cameras, phones
Where the line falls
The same purchase can sit in different buckets depending on why you bought it — the weekly shop is Essentials, the bottle of wine for a dinner party is an Indulgence. Decide once, be consistent, and the monthly totals become comparable.
A cinema subscription is Culture; a night out at the cinema bar is not. Clothes are an Indulgence even when you needed them, unless they're workwear or a child's uniform.
How it works on this page
Delfin already has your transactions, so instead of writing entries by hand you classify your categories once and every day sorts itself. Set a bucket on a top-level category and its subcategories follow; open the mapping to give one of them a bucket of its own.
Each calendar cell shows the day split four ways, each bucket always in the same corner. Click a figure to see the transactions behind it.
Your fixed expenses appear in the day figures too — rent and insurance usually belong in Essentials. They're left out of the pace colour, which watches only day-to-day spending.
The method's own advice, worth keeping: look at the week while it's still running, not once it's over.
